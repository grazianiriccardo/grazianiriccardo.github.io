The main divides

How easily can we tell which side a speaker belongs to from a two-word phrase? The model reports a percentage: 50% is chance. We also show what the model finds when group labels are shuffled at random. The gap between the two is our measure of linguistic partisanship.

How party speech changes

How much does a party’s language differ from the previous period? Each result compares the party’s speakers across two adjacent periods, subtracts the random-label benchmark, and adjusts for the change seen among other parties. Positive values mean greater change than the comparison parties. A change may reflect new speakers, different topics or different wording—not necessarily a shift in political position.

The words that divide

These two-word phrases contribute strongly to the estimated difference between the selected groups. The bars show how often each group uses them per 100,000 phrases. Common words are removed and words are stemmed, so the phrases may look incomplete (“chemin fer” represents “chemin de fer”). Procedural language, routine expressions, numbers, personal names, and names of parties or parliamentary groups are excluded. Frequent use does not by itself indicate support for an idea.

About the measure

The measure follows Gentzkow, Shapiro and Taddy (2019, Econometrica). A penalized statistical model uses two-word phrases to estimate how well a phrase identifies a speaker’s political group within a period. The main-divide estimates adjust for gender and province. We estimate the same model after randomly shuffling group labels to obtain a benchmark for chance and estimation noise. Shaded bands are 80% subsampling intervals, not 95% confidence intervals. An interval crossing zero means the direction of a trend is unclear at that level of uncertainty.

How to interpret the results. A higher score means stronger differences in language, not necessarily greater ideological disagreement. It may reflect subject matter, changes in membership, or differences in wording. Pairwise comparisons are symmetric: they cannot tell which of two parties moved toward the other. Results based on a few periods or multiple comparisons should be read cautiously.

  • Periods group parliamentary sessions (usually around two years). Shaded columns mark limited coverage of sittings in 1832–1843 and 1992–1995. Apparent changes in these years may partly reflect missing records.
  • French and Dutch phrases are analysed separately and combined for the main historical divides. For comparisons between modern parties, Dutch and French are shown separately.
  • Left–right compares socialists and communists with liberals, N-VA and the radical right; Right–others compares the latter with all other parties. Flemish–Walloon compares members of the same parties, in French only, so it ends when the national parties split along language lines (1968–1978). Women–men compares women and men of the same party family.
  • Vertical lines mark plural voting (1894), proportional representation (1900), equal male suffrage (1919), women's suffrage (1949), the split of the national parties (1968–1978) and the federal Chamber (1995).
  • Minimum speakers per side: 20 for the main divides, 15 for party-family and topic comparisons, 10 for individual party pairs, and 8 for the comparisons of today’s parties against the rest. Missing values should not be read as zero.
  • For party pairs, the direct score is symmetric: A versus B equals B versus A. An additional comparison adjusts that score for how far each reference party is from its other parties in the same period, while accounting for pair-specific baselines and whether both are in government or opposition. These reference-party-adjusted comparisons can differ, but cannot identify who changed direction. Separately, the party-change charts compare a party across adjacent periods relative to the changes of other parties.

Data

The dataset uses available official plenary reports of the Belgian Chamber of Representatives, 1831–2025: the Annales parlementaires and, from 1999, the integral reports (CRIV). Text is segmented into speeches and linked to speakers, parties and constituencies where identified. Coverage is incomplete in some historical periods, as noted above.
