Partisanship in Belgian parliamentary speech, 1831–2025

From “Partisanship in Belgian Parliamentary Speech: Evidence from the Chamber of Representatives, 1831–2025”

How the measure works

Take one two-word phrase from a speech. A listener who hears only this phrase, and knows how often each group uses it, can say how likely it is that the speaker belongs to one group or the other. Before hearing anything the odds are 50–50. The score of a period is the probability that the listener gives to the speaker’s true group, averaged over all the phrases spoken. It rises as the two groups use more different words.

A statistical model finds some difference even between groups formed at random. So the same model is estimated again with the group labels shuffled, and its score is subtracted. Most charts show this net partisanship, in percentage points: at +0.50 pp, a phrase moves the listener 0.5 points further towards the true group than it does with shuffled labels. The 80% bands show how much the estimate moves across random subsamples of speakers.

The score measures differences in wording. They can come from the topics debated, the words chosen and the members who speak, and they say nothing directly about how far apart the groups are in their policy positions.

How the estimate is computed
  1. Text. The official plenary reports of the Chamber, 1831–2025: the Annales parlementaires and, from 1999, the integral reports. The text is segmented into speeches, linked to speakers, parties and constituencies where they are identified. Coverage is incomplete in some periods, most of all in 1832–1843 and 1992–1995. Words are stemmed, common words are removed and the text is cut into two-word phrases. Phrases without political content are left out: procedural formulas, courtesies and titles, names of people, parties and groups, amounts and dates. A law with its year and an age can remain, as in “67 ans”.
  2. Groups and periods. Each divide splits the members into two groups, A and B, for example the Catholic family and all the other parties. Speeches are grouped into periods of about two years. French and Dutch have separate vocabularies, and after the national parties split (1968–1978) each party is compared only with parties of its own language. A period needs at least 20 speakers in each group (15 in the comparisons of two party families).
  3. Phrase frequencies. For each phrase, a multinomial logit model estimates how often each group uses it in each period, with gender and province as controls (Gentzkow, Shapiro and Taddy 2019, estimated with the Poisson approximation). A lasso penalty on the group effects, chosen for each phrase by the Bayesian Information Criterion, pulls the group difference of rare phrases towards zero. With raw counts, a phrase used once would look perfectly partisan.
  4. Score. Let qA,j and qB,j be the estimated shares of phrase j in the speech of the two groups. A listener who starts at 50–50 and hears phrase j gives group A the probability
    ρj = qA,j / (qA,j + qB,j).
    Partisanship is the expected probability given to the speaker’s true group:
    π = ½ Σj qA,j ρj + ½ Σj qB,j (1 − ρj),
    averaged over the speakers of the period. It equals 0.5 when the two groups use every phrase equally often. The French and Dutch scores are averaged with weights equal to each language’s share of the phrases.
  5. Benchmark. The whole estimation is repeated after shuffling the group labels among the speakers of each period, with the size of each group kept. Net partisanship is the real score minus this random score.
  6. Uncertainty. For each period, the model is estimated again on 100 subsamples with 20% of the speakers. They give the 80% intervals, which show how much an estimate moves with the speakers included. A change from one period to another would need the uncertainty of the difference, which the intervals of single periods do not give. A period counts as above its benchmark when the lower bound of the real series is above the upper bound of the random series. This is a descriptive rule, and the intervals are not corrected for the many comparisons.
  7. One minute of speech. The probability of guessing the group after about 33 phrases, roughly one minute, treats the phrases as independent.

The paper gives the details in its section “Model and estimation”. Each section of the page has its own notes, in a panel like this one.

Main findings

  1. Catholic and non-Catholic members use similar wording under census suffrage. They become easier to tell apart in the decade after the Socialists enter the Chamber in 1894. Since the early 1990s the religious divide has stayed within its random benchmark (chart).
  2. The left is most distinct from the other parties between the 1890s and 1940, and again in 2024–2025. Government and opposition are most distinct between the wars, and since 1995 their wording is above the benchmark in more than half of the periods (chart).
  3. Even at the peaks of the three main divides, one minute of speech would reveal a speaker’s group with a probability of at most 63–66%. The peaks found for the United States and Finland are higher (comparison).
  4. Votes and words change at different speeds. Coalition partners start to vote alike as soon as they enter government, while their wording changes little more than placebo vocabularies do (event studies).

How to read these estimates

What the score captures. The score measures how distinctive the wording is. Levels and trends can reflect shifts in topic, language, individual speakers, party membership, OCR coverage or the conventions of parliamentary reporting. Comparisons over time describe the series as they are, with all these changes in composition included.

Limits of inference. Most intervals in the charts are speaker-subsampling ranges. Some differences in the question box combine the ranges of single periods as if they were independent, although the same speakers appear in several periods. Many party pairs and events are compared, and the 80% intervals are not adjusted for multiple testing. The event-study section reports which results keep a false discovery rate below 10%.

Linguistic differences within topics

Each speech is assigned to one of 24 topics, grouped into nine policy areas. Each chart compares wording within a topic. Values are net of the random-label benchmark, and the grey series is all speech.

How topics are assigned

A companion module assigns a topic to every speech. A language model (gpt-6-luna) first labels agenda items from a sample of sittings, together with the subject keywords of the legislative files. These labels are grouped into 23 topics and a residual category for procedure. The language model then labels samples of passages of up to 300 words with this codebook. Two classifiers trained on those labels, CamemBERT for French and RobBERT-2023 for Dutch, classify all 3.1 million passages of the corpus. On passages held out from training, they give the language model’s label in 74% of cases. Each speech takes the topic with the highest mean probability across its passages, so a speech that covers several topics is counted under its main one. The 24 categories are grouped into nine policy areas. Partisanship by topic is estimated by legislature within the speeches of each topic or area, with the same model and random-label benchmark as the main series. These estimates have no intervals, so single legislatures are only indicative.

04 / Parliamentary behaviour

Speech, voting and regional divisions

Contrast linguistic distinctiveness with observed votes and electoral geography.

Speech and voting disagreement

Do groups that speak more differently also vote more differently? Here linguistic partisanship is set beside the division in roll-call votes, measured as between-group voting distance: 0 when the two groups vote yes in the same proportion, 1 when they vote unanimously against each other. The correlations describe how the two measures move together.

How roll-call votes are measured

Roll-call votes are read from the same reports, which list the members voting yes, no or abstaining, and are linked to the member register. For each roll call, between-group voting distance is the absolute difference between the two groups’ shares of “yes” votes among the “yes” and “no” votes cast: |YesA/(YesA+NoA) − YesB/(YesB+NoB)|. Abstentions are excluded, and so are roll calls without enough votes for or against in both groups. Zero means identical yes shares, and one means unanimously opposed votes. The period measure averages this distance over the roll calls that qualify. Periods with fewer than 20 roll calls, or with low coverage of the sittings, are left out of the charts and the correlations. The groups are those of the speech series: Catholics, the left, the right and the government against all others, the left against the right, pairs of parties since 1979, women and men of the same party, and, until the national parties split, members of the same party elected in Flemish and in Walloon districts. The paper reports the Catholic, left and government comparisons. The others follow the same rules.

Recorded reactions in the Chamber

This section turns from wording to how members treat each other. It uses what the stenographers noted in the Chamber, disorder (interruptions, noise, protests and uproar) and approval, and the chair’s calls to order. It also counts the references by name from one member to another. The coding of emotions in the speeches themselves, such as anger, is in preparation.

How reactions and references are coded

A module of the project reads the stage directions written by the stenographers: disorder (interruptions, noise, protests and uproar), approval and laughter, and the chair’s calls to order and censures. Rates are per 100,000 words spoken by members, the chair excluded, or per 100 sittings. The module also finds references by name from one member to another, links them to members and ministers, and records whether they cross party families or language communities, compared with the share expected if speakers and targets were paired at random. These measures need no language model and cover the whole period. For the investiture and State of the Union debates since 1961, they give intervention counts close to those of Peeters and Lefever (2026) in most debates and similar shares of references across the language border. The coding of emotions in the speeches (eight categories, such as anger and fear) and of the tone of references (no attack, civil attack, uncivil attack) is in preparation: a language model has labelled samples of passages, two human coders will check part of them, and a multilingual classifier will extend the labels to all speeches.

One minute of speech in Belgium and other parliaments

The one-phrase model can be extended to about 33 phrases, roughly one minute of speech, by treating the phrases as independent, although words in the same speech are correlated. Several studies of other parliaments report the same projection.
